TRAVEL LOG - 20040804

I woke at 8am with plans to leave Bozeman.  I was free to head-out and accumulate more trip adventure, having completed all updates to the website through August 3rd.  Before I could leave for Yellowstone, my next destination, I had to write a few postcards, find a post office for stamps, then replenish my potable water reserves exhaused from 3 days of steady water use.
<p>
Finding a water source proved the most challenging.  Not knowing the lay-of-the-land when it comes to gas stations in Bozeman, I found myself driving hither and yon, looking for a station that had water service.  I finally found an Exxon but I had to fill from a 100 foot hose that was used to irrigate the grounds.  Had I looked much longer, my alternative plan was to knock on someone's door and ask to use their hose.  I suspect before this cross-country trip comes to a conclusion, I will have knocked on a few doors in search of nature's cool refreshment.
<p>
Finally all tasks were completed and I headed out to Yellowstone.  My route took me due east on I-90 then due south on Rt-98 all the way to Mammoth Hot Springs, my first site within the park.  Since it was around noon when I departed I found myself feeling somewhat apprehensive about getting a campsite when I arrived.  July and August are peak season in the park, even on weekdays.
<p>
Jumping onto I-90 I was immediately surprised by the passing scenery, it wasn't half bad. Driving the Interstate is usually a boring experience but the drive proved acceptable in the all important eye-candy category.  While I was marveling at the scenery, I missed my exit.  That happens every once in a while, I get lost in the sights, but I immediately forgave myself and drove to the next exit.  At the exit's stop sign I looked across the way and saw a big sign for an exhibit, the Montana Grizzly Encounters.
<p>
All day up to this point I was feeling a bit rushed.  My concern of missing out on a campsite at Yellowstone was controlling the day's tempo.  But then I got to thinking, so what if I don't get a campsite, I'll find an alternative.  This trip is about finding opportunities and jumping on them.  So I pounced without impunity, I turned right and entered the sight.
<p>
When I was waiting for my turn in line at the entry gate, I had a horrible vision of a helpless, emaciated bear in a cramped, feces-filled cage.  I envisioned people standing around the cage with long, pointy sticks.  All were simultaneously poking and prodding the poor animal, laughing in succession at its distressed, pathetic state.  I hoped at length my vision wasn't true, so after paying the $5 admission, I drove around to the viewing area, wishing all the while my earlier vision wouldn't match reality.
<p>
To my pleasant surprise the exhibit actually turned out to be a wonderful and inspiring dedication to the grizzly bear.  I spent some time admiring the exhibit's mini-ecosystem, reading the multitude of placards, and talking with Bud, the exhibit's Curator and all-around-do-everything guy.
<p>
Click here for more detail (and pictures) on my visit to Montana Grizzly Encounters.
<p>
After departing the exhibit, I backtracked to my missed exit and found myself turning onto a dirt road.  When I plotted the course earlier with my GPS software, I hadn't paid close attention to the routing details.  Apparently the software plotted my course based on shortest distance, not shortest in-transit time.  But I wasn't complaining, it was an opportunity to leave the Interstate and really immerse myself into the sights and sounds of Montana's wilderness.
<p>
The dirt road was about 20 miles long and in fairly good shape.  On most sections I drove 35 mph or so but a few sections had to be driven 15 mph due to periodic onslaughts of washboarding caused by water erosion.  I certainly could have driven faster on the rough sections but the tremulous affect would have really shaken the camper construction and I didn't want to breaking anything.
<p>
After driving a short distance I realized lunch time had arrived so I pulled over and fixed a chicken sandwich.  I opened the van's side doors and enjoyed the view of horses grazing in a large, green pasture.  Yes, this is the life!
<p>
Here are a few views from my dirt road driving stint.
<p>
While driving on the dirt road I encountered many driveway entrance archways.  Well, they aren't exactly archways because they're not curved, rather, in their simplest form they're just two vertical poles with a horizontal pole spanning across top of the two verticals.  When driving your car through them are you supposed to feel like you've entered Shangri-La?  They've always been a mystery to me.  But folks out in these parts really like them, some more than others.
<p>
Some folks are artistic.
<p>
Other folks like to use wood and steel.
<p>
Some folks are open and inviting.
<p>
Other folks have to have a gate to swing shut.
<p>
I wondered if there are hidden meanings in the various designs.  If I ever had to build one it certainly wouldn't have a gate.  I think it would be made of stone and mortar, a material that weathers better than wood.  But that would look funny in Montana where everything seems to be built out of wood.  
<p>
About 10 miles into my dirt road adventure I encountered two horses that looked rather friendly.  I decided to pull over and see if they'd eat some raw carrots I had in my cooler.
<p>
I approached both horses and offered each a carrot.  To my surprise, neither horse was interested.  I thought all horses liked carrots?
<p>
And then I had a little start.  One of the horses was missing an eye!  Initially I hadn't noticed but it soon became clear to me the horse had a disability.  It was weird, the socket was all covered with skin.  So I got to wondering if maybe it was born without an eye?  Aside from that the horse looked healthy and happy.  The owner/rancher is a pretty good guy/gal, didn't put the horse down because of it's disability.
<p>
"Wise Acres", thought that was funny.  Do the Wises live there?
<p>
Near the tail-end of my dirt road driving I saw an opportunity for a panoramic picture of some mountains in the distance.  I was disappointed after exiting the van because a telephone line was strung across the view.  So the only thing I could do was to take to the high ground.
<p>
I climbed through a barbed wire fence then started my ascent.  On the way up I saw many cactus and even a jack rabbit.
<p>
I then encountered pile upon pile of horse manure.
<p>
So much manure that I had to be careful I didn't step in any.  I was literally hiking up a hill in Nowhere, MT and would have been surprised to see two piles, let along hundreds.  So were all the hillsides covered w/horse droppings?
<p>
And I even encountered a few cow plops during my climbing.
<p>
This poor cactus got really spanked with a horse pile.  Surprised it's still alive.  Too much fertilizer can't be a good thing.
<p>
As I climbed the clouds overhead started to turn dark and ominous.  I wanted to climb all the way to the top but heard rumbles of thunder so I stopped climbing and readied myself for some picture taking.
<p>
Looking down at the van, it seemed really far away.
<p>
Here's the vista I had to work for, paid with hill climbing sweat and horse manure danger.
<p>
As I descended the hill I got to wondering, what is the range on my alarm system?  So I gave it a test, pushing the red panic button and to my surprise the alarm went off.  I was tempted to climb back up the hill to try it from a further distance but the clouds above reminded me with a clap of thunder that I needed to go down, not up.
<p>
Before I climbed back through the barbed wire fence I checked my sneakers for any unwelcome stowaways.  Upon close inspection none were found so I hurried through the fence and back to the van before it started to sprinkle.  And in Montana when it sprinkles it sprinkles big rain drops.  I'd hate to be caught in a Montana downpour!
<p>
So a little more driving and I was back on pavement.
<p>
Gardiner, MT was just ahead.  It's a bit of a tourist trap, there were a lot out outfitters selling services, mostly raft tours.  Also many restaurants and tourist related business lined both sides of the street.
<p>
The north entrance to Yellowstone is just outside of Gardiner.  It's quite spectacular.  See, that's the kind of entrance I'd build, one out of stone and mortar.  So I guess such entrances are acceptable in Montana, assuming you're a national park of course.
<p>
At the gate the campground vacancy status-board (sorry, it's blurry) said the only campground open was the first one, Mammoth Hot Springs.  It was going on 6pm and I was really pushing my luck.  If I couldn't get a campsite I'd have to exit the park because they don't allow car-camping just anywhere.
<p>
FYI you cannot molest animals in the park.  So how does one molest an animal?
<p>
On the way to the campground I pass across the 45th parallel.  The sign was right next to two parking lots filled with cars so I stopped to find out what all the hubbub was.  I started thinking about that experiment where you drain a sink and the water doesn't spin left or right, it just drains without spinning.  But "that's an equator thing" I remembered so all this activity was something else.
<p>
I managed to catch someone on the way to their car and they indicated there was a swimming hole about 1/2 mile down the way.  I thought about investigating but remembers I had a campsite to catch so back in the truck and up the hill I went.
<p>
I've arrived.
<p>
Campground sign doesn't say "full" yet so there is a chance there are still vacancies.
<p>
Geeze, the campground hosts are living-life-large...  That's a pretty nice RV, even has the pull out sides.
<p>
I pulled the van up registration and yes, they had a site for me.  As I was filling out my registration they started turning people away because the campsite had filled.  Had I arrived one minute later I would have been turned away. Boy, that was a close one.
<p>
I jumped back into the truck and drive to my site, #66.  On the way, I got to thinking about why this campground was the last to fill.  You'd think it'd be the first to fill because it's near both the entrance and Mammoth Hot Springs, one of the parks main attractions.  Then I thought about Mexican border towns, like Tiajuana.  Was there a parallel going on here at Yellowstone?  This was a "border campground"?  As I drove to my campsite I could see why.  The sites were small and exposed.  Most folks who were staying had RVs.
<p>
My site was okay, it had the basics, nothing more, nothing less.  Not that it really mattered.  I paid $14 dollars to sleep in my van, oh well, at least I'm in the park!
<p>
After fixing dinner and eating, I high-tailed it back to the 45th parallel to see what the swimming hole was all about.
<p>
No wonder why there were 2 parking lots serving the attraction.  The swimming hole was a natural outdoor Jacuzzi!  Too bad it was almost 8:45 but at least I'd get a few minutes in the water.  Plus I needed a bath anyway.
<p>
After a 1/2 mile hike...
<p>
...I reached the attraction.  There were a lot of people enjoying themselves in Gardiner River, some were splashing around but most were sitting, soaking up to their necks.
<p>
Gardiner River, the main river, was being fed by the Boiling River that originates right out of the ground.
<p>
Just downriver of where the Boiling River flowed into the Gardiner River, some wise soul had built up a wall of rocks to help trap the hot water and form a sort of Jacuzzi.  It was really a wonderful thing.  I only stayed in for about 15 minutes but thought I could always come back again before I leave the park.
<p>
9pm had arrived so I climbed out of the water and walked back to the van as the sun was setting.
<p>
When I got back to my campsite I worked on my website for a while then went to bed.

PEOPLE - Bud

I met Bud at Montana Grizzly Encounters, a grizzly bear exhibit located off I-90 near Chestnut, MT.  At the gate I was told to talk to Bud if I had any questions.  Bud turned out to be the Exhibit's Curator and resident jack-of-all-trades.  After looking over the impressive exhibit I asked him about their breeding policies and from there Bud proved to be a walking, talking encyclopedia of bear information.
<p>
Apparently he stumbled onto the position.  He was working with the concrete contractor who ended up not finishing the job.  Bud pulled a Superman and saved the day, stepped in and not only finished the concrete job, he also pretty much designed and built the whole mini-ecosystem for the bears.  From there he stayed on as Curator.
<p>
Originally from Iowa, Bud moved out to Montana in 1995.  In the past he's worked many jobs including long-haul trucking and carnival setup and ride operation.  He's traveled all over the country and when asked where he'd like to travel in the future he told me he wants to just stay put in Montana.  He loves Montana even tough it only has 3 seasons which he humorously described as "last winter, this winter, and next winter".  One dream of his is to retire somewhere deep in the Montana mountains and live off the land.

SIGHTS - Montana Grizzly Exhibit

Located off I-90 near Chestnut, MT, Montana Grizzly Encounters provides a unique opportunity to to get up-close-and-personal with 3 bears of the grizzly persuasion.  The $3 million dollar attraction was build over a period of 2 years with private funds.  The exhibit operates as a non-profit and was opened to the public in May of 2004.
<p>
I stumbled upon the attraction while in the process of backtracking from a missed exit.  As I pulled off the exit on my way back, I saw the sign announcing the bear exhibit and thought I'd check it out.  After paying my $5 admission fee, I pulled around to the viewing area that was filled with people sitting and watching the bears.
<p>
The mini-ecosystem was impressive, providing room for up to 4 bears.
<p>
It had a torrential water fall.
<p>
Many placards were on display, providing interesting and educational information about bears.
<p>
During my visit Brutus and Sheena were both on display.  Brutus, 2 years old at 411 lbs and Sheena, 18 years old at 263 lbs, were meandering around foraging for cherries.  Bears in the wild live for about 25 years, living in captivity will add another 10 years.  Brutus will more than double in weight when he reaches adulthood and will stand around 7.5 feet tall.  Sheena has reached her full size and weight.
<p>
Earlier one of the owners was sprinkling around cherries, a way to get the bears out of their dens and into public view.
<p>
Here is Sheena foraging for cherries.
<p>
Here is Brutus taking a drink.
<p>
Brutus and Sheena together.  All the bears are fixed so no little ones are in their futures.  The exhibit only takes bears that are in perl or bears that have been raised in captivity (thus losing their ability to live in the wild).  They believe to breed the animal for display purposes is cruel.
<p>
The bears are on their way out, up and over the hill.  They were being called back so the owner could safely go out and distribute cherries.
<p>
I met up with Bud, the exhibit's Curator and jack-of-all-trades.  He proved to be an encyclopedia of interesting bear facts and figures.
<p>
The attraction has future plans for improvement and expansion.  They want make it more realistic and have plans to cover both sides of the concrete wall with granite.  They also plan to erect a building that'll house more bears to effectively increase the number of bears they can have at the facility.
<p>
Since the exhibit operates as a non-profit, in addition to the entrance fee they also take donations.
<p>
I was really happy I missed my exit and stumbled across this attraction.  They've done a really nice job tending to the bear's needs and providing the public with a way to safely get close to the bears in a non-zoo setting.  I really enjoyed my visit and urge you to pay the bears a visit and support the facility.
<p>
My experience at Montana Grizzly Encounters gets a solid 9 out of 10.
