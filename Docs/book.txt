It's two hours past midnight, I'm weary, and I'm far, far away from home.  The neighborhood I slowly pass through is 
completely silent, nothing is sturring, even the wind has long since stirred its last leaf.  In the street's dark margins, nestled deep within their warm, comfortable beds, the folks of this Chicago suburb are both fast asleep and oblivious to my current wanderings.  Well, at least that's my hope as I purposefully creep along in my search.  Somewhere within these lonely streets I will find much needed refuge.  I am seeking sleep, much needed sleep, but with one small requirement. I must feel extra secure where I slumber tonight, and based upon a number of past, unpleasant experiences encountered on my cross-country trip so far, that instinct couldn't be any stronger.  The time is now at hand to find that secret parking place, a small, hidden spot where I can street-camp in peace.  I'm tired, I'm starting to fall asleep at the wheel, and worst of all, I've got to go to the bathroom.

A good measure of driving fails to serve up that optimal parking spot I'd been hoping for; too many bright street lights and too few cars parked on the street where I can blend in and hide.  Sufficiently frustrated, I drive no further.  Recklessly casting my fate into the wind, I quickly pick a spot, park, then promptly kill the engine and lights.  The sweet presence of silence is now upon me.  I sit still in my seat for a while, listening and looking intently for any sound or movement within the street's dark borders, praying that no one has taken notice of my sudden appearance.  It's times like this when I wish my van was painted a dark color, a color that would easily blend into the night.  Instead, the van is painted a blinding white.  In these post-9/11 times, perhaps it's calling out to the suspicious masses, exclaiming, "Hey, look at me!  I'm a white van!  I don't belong here!  Are you wondering what's inside me?  Perhaps a terrorist?  Maybe a fertilizer bomb, just like the one that brought down the Murrah Federal Building in Oklahoma City?"  Regardless of what the paranoid might think, she's a good, reliable van, and so far, she hasn't let me down.  So, white or not, I'm sticking with her, through thick and thin, strife and peril.

I continue my stationary vigil.  Five minutes go by and nothing transpires, other than me feeling a bit silly for feeling so paranoid.  Convinced I've succeeded in bagging a safe place to slumber, I slowly slide out of the driver's seat, past my privacy curtain, and into the back of the van and out of view.  Certainly I'll have no problems street-camping here tonight.  Before going to bed, I must, however, attend to the naggings of Mother Nature.  Sparing you the torrid details of how I handle going to the bathroom on the road, I'll just say I'm sitting on my little white waste basket when something bad happens.  And I mean really bad.  As I sit there, I hear one car, and then another.  As the second car slowly passes by, it shines a bright spotlight upon the side of the van.  Sitting upright on my little throne and feeling rather exposed to the searching light, I immediately hit the floor.  A lightening strike of fear stabs into me, for I am now a desperate man in an even more desperate position.  All I can do is pray that whoever is outside will quickly lose interest and move on.  That is my only hope.

Scrambling, I tidy things up as best as I can.  Unfortunately, I don't have time to do the necessary paperwork that nature's calling requires.  Damn!  Feeling rather panicked and humiliated at this point, to my absolute horror, someone starts to knock at my van's side doors.  Oh God...! should I just ignore it?  Or should I answer it and give my visitors a little sample of the aroma that's currently wafting around within my little home on wheels?  Not wishing to make matters worse, I pull up my pants, attempt to wipe the embarrassment off my face, then slowly open the side doors.  As my view of the outside unfolds, all I can do is hope for the best...

===== Trip Reason =====

Ahhh, the joys of traveling on-the-cheap.  But, let's not get ahead of ourselves, shall we?  Let me pull you back to the beginning of my story.  It's a story about a journey comprised of many things; people, places, revelations, love, joy, fear, exhilaration.  And every journey, whether big or small, has both a starting point and a reason for taking place.  On July 8, 2004 my voyage begins when I pushed away from the dock and head north into Washington State from Portland, Oregon, my hometown.  The trip itself is a wopper, a 6-month cross-country tour of the United States.  Why the big trip?

As they sometimes say, set free what you love, and if by chance it comes back to you, it's yours forever.  Well, I suppose the same could be said of one's life.  Holding on too tightly will only make you miserable, closed-minded, self-centered, and perhaps even worse, back you into the unpleasant corner of no return.  Finding myself somewhat unhappy with the jigsaw life I've assembled over my 39 years of life, I come to terms, after long denying the obvious fact, that some of my puzzle pieces are altogether missing and others are jammed into spaces where they just don't belong.  Standing back and looking at my puzzle creation, I cannot, for the life of me, make out what it is.  So where has this life of mine taken me?  Honestly, I'm not at all that sure.  Is this a mid-life crisis I'm going through?  Maybe.  Perhaps it's time to scatter the pieces of my life in an effort to reconstruct and rediscover?  Nothing short of giving up everything for a fresh start, is this the only way?

Travel has a nack of shaking loose life's cobwebs through new experiences.  It's a great way of breaking away from the status quo, to bring to life new adventures that can only be gained by leaving the comforts and familiarities of one's own stomping grounds.  I'm 39 years old, and I'm a little ashamed to say, I'm not well-traveled.  I never took the time to strike out and see the world.  Always too busy with work and focused on local interests, it's been really easy to let time pass me by, repeatedly making and breaking promises to myself that next year I'll take that big trip to wherever doing whatever.  Without the proper planning and thought, sadly, that time will never come for me.  How many times have you heard the story about the guy/gal who worked every day of their life and never took a vacation, declaring that plenty of time for travel will come after retirement.  Retirement comes, tragedy soon follows (death, sickness), and the travel never happens, the expanded of horizons are sadly unrealized.

==== Trip Routing =======

The width and breadth of the United States is enormous and cannot be adequately experienced by reading a book or even flying in an airplane.  One must actively move across its surface, whether walking, running, biking, or, in my case, driving.  For such a lengthy adventure, the choice of travel by vehicle is the popular one, and one that fits well into my plans.  Racing against time before the cold weather sets in, given my early July departure date, I wish to circumvent the United States in about 4 months.

I quickly decide my trip planning will be somewhat free and loose, allowing for serendipity wherever it finds me.  Traveling alone certainly has its advantages in planning.  The only debate in sightseeing venue and route selection will take place solely within my mind, and since I'm fairly sure I'm not scitsofrinic, all arguments should prove both orderly and short lived.  Additionally, any plans made can be easily changed down the road with a single vote, cast from my footloose and fancy-free one citizen democracy.  In support of a simple 50,000 feet travel plan, I purchase two large maps of the United States.  One to be tacked up on my corkboard at home and the other taken with me on the trip.

Being a fairly logical person, I set to task and breakdown my trip down into two components.  What's a trip primarily composed of?  Well, in a nutshell, a trip all is about meeting people and seeing sights.  Right?  First off, with a box of color stickpins in hand, I stick my corkboard mounted map with pins, each designating the location of all my family and friends.  Next, I look closely at the map and search for interesting places to visit, including parks, monuments, cities, landmarks, forests, whatever catches my attention and piques my interest.  Into all the places of interest I stick a pin.  Green stickpins are placed in parks, yellow stickpins in the monuments, red stickpins in cities, etc.

After my little flurry of pinning is complete, I stand back to visualize a clockwise route around the country, starting and ending in Portland, Oregon.  Similar to the kids game of connect-the-dots, I proceed to connect-the-pins and soon enough, a jogging, dizzying image emerges.   It's a bird, it's a plane, no, it's Super Trip!  When the dust finally settles and my routing is fully absorbed, my connect-the-pins exercise produces a trip plan that stretches northward to Vancouver, BC, eastward to the Maine Coast, southward to southern Texas, and westward to the California Coast.  Within a matter of an hour, I have my trip all figured out!  Whoever said that trip planning is a difficult task never utilized the map and stickpin method.

===== Pre-Trip Revelation ======

I really love to bike.  Since learning to ride my first bicycle way back when bike seats were banana-shaped and braking required the rider to peddle backwards, my appreciation for the two-wheeled sport has grown over time as I slowly graduated from single-speed to three-speed to ten-speed to twenty-one-speed.  Over time, a mountain bike was finally thrown into the mix, effectively rounding out my bicycling experience to where I can ride pratically anywhere, on-road and off.  Not only is biking a pleasure for me, it's also a challenge.  I like a good challenge, and one way I feed that need is through biking.

After purchasing a new road bike in 2003 with best intentions of seriously getting into road racing, a year goes by and nothing happens.  It's so easy to push things off until next year I've found.  Whether it's getting a new job, taking up that new hobby, whatever.  The root cause for my putting things off is always the same, namely, laziness.  In the spring of 2004 the scale fortunately tips away from procrastination to action.  With the arrival of the first nice day of the year in rainy Portland, Oregon, I pose myself a grand challenge.  I will conscribe the services of my new road bike and ride it as far as I can go in a single day.  Having not ridden a bike in over 9 months and not being in particulary good shape to begin with, I'll be pleased if I can bike 50 miles, however, 30 miles or less is more likely.

In all fairness to myself due to my current level of fitness, I elect to ride on flat terrain.  Sauve Island near Portland, Oregon fits that bill perfectly, offering a nice 11-mile loop with the side benefit of nice country scenery.  I will repeatedly ride the loop until I run out of daylight or energy, whichever comes first.  With plan in hand, I drive out to Sauve Island with a cooler full of food and drink, enough to fuel my two-wheeled endeavor.

After donning my camelpack and stuffing my biking shirt with a fist-full of energy bars, I set off on my long biking adventure.  It's such a nice day and I'm feeling pretty strong during the first mile of my ride, that's no reason to believe anytime soon I'll be getting tired or perhaps saddle sore.  No sir!  So, on I ride.  The first 11-mile loop is cake, the second circuit is still relatively easy, and the third circuit is not so easy because my butt is getting sore and my legs are feeling a bit tired.  At the completion of the third circuit (33 miles, yeah!!) I stop at the car to refuel.  After a short rest, I start on the fourth circuit and feel pretty good out the gate.  Near the tail end of the fourth circuit my left knee starts to hurt.  Should I stop?  Hell no!  So, on I go to the fifth circuit.  In a nutshell, my knee starts to hurt more on the fifth circuit, on the sixth my left hamstring feels a little pulled, on the seventh I'm really start to run out of energy, on the eighth I seriously consider stopping at 88 miles.  Hmmm...  I'll only have 12 miles to go to hit 100!  I decide, come hell or high water, I'm going for the century!  When the dust finally settles and I dismount the saddle, I manage to ride 100 miles!  Yes, a 100 miles.  I've never ridden 100 miles.  To top it off, I'm not in good shape.

Feeling rather empowered for having far exceeded my bike ride's expectations, I have a big revelation on the drive home.  If I can ride 100 miles without any training, what could I do with a little training?  Maybe I could bike ride across the country?!?  Now, that idea lasts for about 30 seconds.  I quickly snap back to reality and realize I really don't want to bike across the country.  Riding 100 miles in a day is one thing, but the United States is more than 2700 miles wide as the crow flies, and it's definitely not all flat, unlike my ride today.  However, I am hungry for another challenge.  Then it comes to me out of the blue.  I will drive across the country, and with me I will bring along my road and mountain bike.  The trip will be a theme trip, one centered around bike riding venues.  Ha!  I believe I've found my next challenge.

Have you ever been to Montana?  Montana has an interesting nack of grabbing your attention and putting certain things into perspective. At least that's been my experience so far as I pass through this grand state with wide open spaces and omnipresent sky.  In a good portion of this place, with every turn of my neck, I am served up with pretty much the same bland plate of scenery.  I find an expansive, flat landscape spanning out in all directions. === Home is where the van is ===

You may be surprised to learn you really don't need a whole lot to live well.  And it's those folks who have a lot who, in some respects, miss out and actually have less.  What?  How could that be?  Well, let me explain.  Let's start off by saying I used to be a fat onion.  An onion?  Yes, an onion.  And my van, well, it was and still to is to this day a long and well-sharpened knife.

Previous to my cross-country trip, I've always lived in a house, and as a result, I had become somewhat soft and spoiled.  Spoiled?  Well, most people I know live in either house, condo, or apartment, that's just where people hang their hats.  Because such nesting practices are so common, people look at such a living arrangements as the expected norm, one they naturally gravitate towards and, over time, eventually take for granted.  There are other living arrangement options of course, like setting up house in a commune, perhaps in a tent, maybe under a bridge, or in my case, for 6 months, in a vehicle.

At the outset of my trip I was rather uncertain how I'd cope with living out of my van.  All of the amenities and conveniences of home that I had, for so long, taken for granted were quickly stripped away as I first set foot into my van's side doors on the grand day of my trip's glorious departure.  No shower, no flush toilet, no multiple rooms to walk around in, no spacious closet, no fireplace, no washer and dryer, no hot water, you name it, it was either missing or greatly constrained in my new steel-boxed home on wheels.  Imagine taking your abode and shrinking it down into a three-dimentional space of 11' x 5.5' x 4'.  Not an easy task, by any stretch of the imagination.

As I start my trip, my needs for house and home are quite large, not unlike an enormous, multi-layered onion.  Layer upon layer of expectations and comforts comprise it. With some effort, I manage to shoehorn my oversized burden into the van and set sail on my trip, all the while unsure how I'll cope with my new living arrangements.  Unbeknownst to me, the van slowly works its knife paring magic on me.  Over time, it widdles back layer upon layer of my onion.  I soon realize I can take a washcloth bath in lieu of a shower.  As it turns out, the world is my bathroom, providing ample opportunities, from formal facilities offered by gas stations and restaurants to the gloriously informal offerings I find outside in special, secret places.  If I need to stretch out, I jump outside the van.  What better place to stretch!  I have the sky as my ceiling and walls and the ground below me as my floor.  The air is fresher than inside and the scenery is ever changing as I make my way around the country.  Water?  Not a problem.  My 10 gallons of reserve can last me more than a week if I'm mindful to conserve it.  From my college days I know that washing clothes at a laundary mat can be a drag.  However, wearing my clothes over and over again works well, just as long as I don't start to smell.  I can go two to three weeks without doing laundary.

Thanks to the subtle influences of my van, my onion grows smaller and smaller, and I actually grow fonder and fonder of my simplified and greatly downsized living arrangements.  Within less than a month the van has evolved from an uncomfortable inconvenience to a comfortable and secure niche of choice.  I begin to revel in its simplicity, its efficiency, its economics, its resourcefulness.  Looking back at past living arrangements, most recently being a spacious home with a spacious yard, I gain a healthy perspective that more isn't always better.  And in certain situations, far less is better, much better.  Time spent mowing the lawn, fixing that broken pipe, working hard to pay for the costly mortgage.  All this time spent just maintaining a certain status of living (not to be confused with quality of living) perhaps could be better focused and used to participate in more important activities that feed the soul and foster better personal growth and experience.  Stepping out of the box and investigating alternative styles of living has a way of providing revelations.  Living out of a van may seem idiotic to most, but, having experienced it first hand and being in a good position to compare it to the common alternative, it provides one with a large variety of options, options that are not readily available to most because they are mentally and/or financially chained to house and home.

=== FREE INTERNET CONNECTIONS MADE EASY ===

With computer laptop at the ready, it's one thing to sit down in a public place to surf the airwaves in search of a fast and free Internet connection.  It's wholly another matter to purposefully drive around in a van and systematically troll for an Internet connection, sometimes joyfully finding oneself driving a 6-lane information highway and other times lost and going through withdrawal, despirately searching for that poorly marked information dirt road just as an alcoholic might rifle through the liquor cabinet, only to discover it has been emptied.  In most cases, my luck typically falls in-between these two extremes.

Just the act of driving slowly in a neighborhood, any neighborhood, is a magnet for suspicion.  Add to that the fact that my vehicle is a white, extended Ford van with window treatments meant to foil the searching eyes of the curious, and you get a good recepie for rousting the paranoid, or worse, having the cops called on you.  But, with experience in these matters, one gains good skill sets at finding safe bastions on internet plenty, all with the benefit of not being noticed.  Well, at least that is the goal, which is usually successfully reached.  There's always exceptions.  However, if you can bat 95% or better, you are doing very well in my book.

How does one bolster the odds of finding an internet connection as quickly as possible?  Wishing to always shoot lots of fish in a barrel is an unreasonable expectation, assuming you wish to see more that just the inner confines of large, technically advanced cities, like Seattle, WA or San Francisco, CA.  Through trial and error, I eventually "get it" in terms of maximizing my chances of capturing that Internet fish which may be the only one in the barrel.

A couple of maxims exist.  The first, stay clear of poor neighborhoods.   In such neighborhoods, there's little to no opportunities to grab the Internet because the vast majority of the inhabitents are not linked to the Information Highway.  Due to economics, few can't afford to pay the toll of admission.  Additionally, you may run the risk of theft and/or bodily injury if you unwittingly gain their attention and you look like a sucker waiting to be taken advantage of.  This all assumes you wish to avoid all such situations.  I'm sure there are some folks out there who consider themselves "extreme Internet trollers" who get a big thrill out of such challenges.  More power to them.

The next maxim.  Avoid really rich neighborhoods.  These folks are typically very paranoid where you really run the risk of having the cops call on you jiffy quick.  Additionally, the rich can afford to pay a professional to install their wireless network and,  as a result, their networks are typically secure (ie, encrypted) and unless you know the special 128-bit encrypted password, you are plum out of luck.  Avoid this situation, steer clear of the high-end neighborhoods.  The caviot to all this centers around the technical coolness of the city.  Places like Seattle, WA are one of the most connected cities in the United States.  In such cities, the well off appear to be pretty generous about sharing their internet connections.  I found this to be the case, so much so, that in one parking spot in an afluent Seattle neighborhood, I was able to successfully connect to the Internet on 4 separate services.  How cool is that!

Next maxim, the best bang for your buck is to stick with middle class neighborhoods.  Such places are usually well connected and most folks install there own wireless equipment.  Most get stumped by the complexities of making their wireless networks secure so they just give up.  Additionally, if the router equipment they've installed is ActionTec, 9 times out of 10 you're pretty much guarenteed a good connection.  Setting up ActionTec encryption must be more complicated than, say, NetGear.  If you see NetGear, don't bother.  They always seem to be encrypted.

Another trick of the trade is when searching for that internet connection, never, ever drive on the same street twice.  If you are seen on the street driving past slowly just once, when you pass out of sight, you are out of mind.  Do it twice or more and the paranoia bells and whistles may very well go off.

Another tactic that's also good when searching for that perfect street-camping spot is finding streets where many cars are parked.  Parking for long periods of time while surfing the Internet is alway best in the midst of other vehicles.  Like a fish within a school, you'll blend in and your trolling intents will be effectively chemaphlashed.

If you plan on staying in a place (town, city, whatever) for an extended period of time, take care not to repeatedly visiting the same internet spot.  The only exception to this rule is if you've parked your vehicle and don't move it.  If you do that, the neighborhood will think you are visiting someone there.  My experience has been if you park without moving the vehicle, you can stretch your stay up to about 3 days.  Thereafter, if the cops don't get called, your own paranoia will probably grow to the point where you feel a strong need to find another location.  Do you self a big favor, act on your paranoia.

When you finally find that perfect Internet connection and all appears to be safe and secure, don't make the mistake of thinking no one has taken notice of your presence.  In the extreme case, if someone is brave enough to approach your vehicle to investigate, be afraid, be very afraid.  Experience has taught me that no amount of explaining your situation works; showing them an ID, giving them a business card, telling them your travel website URL so they can investigate the validity of your story.  This will get you nowhere.  If someone asks what you're doing, tell them anything other than what you are actually doing.  I don't normall promote lying, however, extreme situations require creative tactics.  Saying you're lost is a good one.  And since people usually like to be helpful, they'll brighten-up, drop their paranoia, then give you directions.  Once you get the directions, leave the neighborhood immediately.  I once made the mistake of thinking a truthful explanation was all that was necessary to mitigate the tenuous nature of my Internet grabbing situation.  After the initial encounter and subsequent interrogation, I stayed put.  After about 30 minutes of uploading new content to my website, I finished my Internet activities, then pull out of the neighborhood.  No sooner do I get three blocks when I notice in my side-view mirror two cop cars behind me with flashing lights.  Not at all fun.  So, lesson learned, get the directions and run!

Most outlying places, like small villages and the like, are typically unfertile ground for the Internet.  Use your instincts on this one, if the community seems stuck in time, where there's only a main street and a couple of side streets at best, don't waste your time searching for a connection, move on.  At best, within such places the Internet is gained via telephone modem and someone using such antiquated technology certainly isn't going to be broacasting their signal wirelessly.  As for residences out in the country, don't bother.  Even if high-speed Internet connections are avaiable, there are too few of them to choose from.  It's always best to increase your odds by trolling communities a little larger than village size.  I guess you'd call that a town.  Consider the low-end in your community search selection a small town.

Business are usually not the greatest feeding ground for free Internet.  Like the rich, businesses either hire professionals to install their wireless network or they're smart enough to install it correctly themselves.  And doing it correctly, in the minds of many, is shutting out trollers like myself through encryption.  Fortunately, the challenges of gaining access to the Internet through business connections is getting easier and easier by the day.  Businesses, like coffee shops, bars, and restaurants, are finally seeing the advantages of offering free WiFi to their customers.  So, grabbing the internet from businesses isn't a bust, however, you need to be selective in choosing your hunting grounds.

If all else fails and you cannot find a free internet connection, there's always places like Starbucks who offer T-Mobile Internet services.  Without a contract, for something like $6 an hour, you can connect.  Now, that's pretty lame in my opinion, considering it's not costing Starbucks much to provide the service.  The only other option is to keep searching, and like hunting for prey, part of the fun is in the pursuit.  Is this a primal instinct that has adapted well to run free in the high-tech world?  Maybe...


=== COLD WEATHER TRAVEL SURVIVAL ===

When the weather turns cold and nasty, folks typically scoot inside to escape the unwelcome and sometimes peralis weather craftily cooked up by Mother Nature.  Like food and drink, the need for shelter is high up in the list of life's necessities.  Such inside shelter is usually a structure of sorts, a building containing infrastructure to produce heat on demand, which is delivered both efficiently and conveniently.  For me, my inside shelter is my van.  During the tail-end of my travels across the United States, I find myself in Colorado at the end of November, high up in the mountains.  High altitude, coupled with November weather, is a perfect recepie for somewhat chilly nights.  Certainly not as chilly as in, say, January, but chilly just the same.

When my trip started way back in early July, the plan was to complete my clockwise romp around the country in 3 months.  At the time, 90 days seemed like a reasonable estimate.  It wasn't like I was walking or biking my route, a van was my mode of travel, certainly 90 days should be sufficient time.  At my trip's 3 month mark I find myself at the Maine Coast and only a 3rd of the way through my trip.  Time had slipped by so quickly, and now, to my distress, the cold weather was on its way.  Picking up my travel pace, one month later I am in Colorado.  Having lost the race against Mother Nature, she is intent in rubbing my face in my defeat with the season's first snowfall.
