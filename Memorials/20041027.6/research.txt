Plans went forward however, and for Pope however the most fitting type of design was clear. The style would be one that Jefferson himself used for Monticello and the University of Virginia. It would be a circular domed design based upon the Pantheon in Rome, which Jefferson believed to be a perfect model of a circular building. In 1936, Pope submitted his design to the commission, and after considerable thought the memorial was accepted. At last, a memorial fitting to Thomas Jefferson, one of our greatest founding fathers, would soon grace the federal city.

Controversy
-------------

 The design approved in 1936 was an adaptation of John Russell Pope's plans for a Theodore Roosevelt Memorial. Favored by the Commission of Fine Arts, this design consisted of an open colonnade with a central sculpture. Although the Thomas Jefferson Memorial Commission had long preferred the Pantheon-type memorial suggested by the McMillan Commission of 1901, they reluctantly agreed to this open-colonnade memorial.


The death of John Russell Pope in 1937 ended the fragile truce between the Commission of Fine Arts and the Thomas Jefferson Memorial Commission. Pope's widow was brought into the struggle, she objected to a memorial retread.

As a result, the Thomas Jefferson Memorial Commission selected one of Pope's earlier Pantheon designs, which was then modified by Otto R. Eggers and Daniel P. Higgins. This infuriated the Commission of Fine Arts and others who opposed the Pantheon-type design and also the continuing involvement of Pope's former associates. The Thomas Jefferson Memorial Commission bypassed this opposition by going to President Franklin D. Roosevelt and getting his approval for their plans. He authorized the altered Pantheon design, giving his full support to the Memorial Commission. This rendered the Commission of Fine Arts powerless to stop the construction of a Pantheon-type memorial for Thomas Jefferson.

Naturalist
------------

There is not a sprig of grass that shoots uninteresting to me."

Thomas Jefferson, a nature lover? Jefferson was passionate about America's independence, but it may be surprising to discover that he was passionate about America's plants, birds, and animals too.

At no time was Jefferson's attachment to the outdoors clearer than on July 4, 1776. That's right, on the morning of our country's birthday Jefferson was not occupied with politics; instead, he was outside noting the temperature, along with the wind direction and precipitation. Such weather observations were a part of Jefferson's routine every morning. Shortly after rising, he was busy watching Mother Nature and recording meticulously her wonders in his journal. Even from indoors, Jefferson could see the weathervane situated on top of his Monticello home. By the way, the birthday of the United States had a pleasant start--68 F and a southeasterly breeze were the conditions in Philadelphia, according to Thomas Jefferson's notes.
